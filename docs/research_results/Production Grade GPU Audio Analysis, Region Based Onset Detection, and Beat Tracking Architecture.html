Production-Grade GPU Audio Analysis, Region-Based Onset Detection, and Beat Tracking Architecture

The deployment of real-time audio analysis within a high-performance compute shader pipeline imposes rigid constraints on signal processing design. In a live performance environment operating at display refresh rates (~60 Hz) fed by asynchronous audio hops (~187 Hz), audio reactivity must be deterministic, surgically isolated, and immune to dynamic drift. The existing cryo_pulse module established a baseline for spectral flux, adaptive thresholding, and autocorrelation tempo tracking. However, its structural reliance on fixed Mel-band splits, global threshold floors, and uncalibrated adaptive thresholding revealed critical limitations—most notably the inability to isolate broadband percussive instruments such as snares and the occurrence of tempo tracking hallucinations during quiet passages.

This report formulates the definitive architectural specification for the next-generation audio analysis engine within Sentinel. By evaluating signal representations, magnitude-only onset detection functions (ODFs), multi-feature region-based instrument isolation, parallelized GPU tempo induction, and causal beat-phase tracking, this specification establishes a mathematically grounded and computationally efficient roadmap.

Input Representation Strategy and Signal Preprocessing

Spectral Representation: Linear Spectrum versus Perceptual Mel Bands

The selection of the input representation dictates the upper bound of instrument discrimination. Sentinel provides two primary spectral representations: a 1024-bin linear magnitude Spectrum and a 138-band Mel Bands array.

The physical parameters measured on Sentinel 0.5.49 establish that the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) bin width is governed by fres​=Nfft​fs​​, where fs​=48000 Hz. At Nfft​=4096, the linear bin resolution is exactly 11.71875 Hz/bin, but the 1024 reported bins span only 0 to 12 kHz. At Nfft​=2048, the linear bin resolution widens to 23.4375 Hz/bin, covering 0 to 24 kHz.

The trade-off between Nfft​=4096 and Nfft​=2048 represents a fundamental limit in time-frequency localization:

  1. Low-Frequency Discrimination (0−500 Hz): Resolving the fundamental frequencies of a kick drum (40−80 Hz) versus the low-mid body of a snare drum (150−250 Hz) or floor tom (90−140 Hz) requires fine spectral resolution. At Nfft​=4096, the kick drum fundamental spans 3 to 4 discrete linear bins, permitting precise regional bounding. On the 138-band Mel scale, low frequencies are heavily compressed; the entire range from 40 Hz to 250 Hz occupies fewer than 15 bands, causing severe spectral smearing between kick and snare energy.

  2. High-Frequency Air Band (12−24 kHz): Percussive transients (such as hi-hat attacks, cymbal sizzle, and snare wire snap) release significant energy above 12 kHz. Truncating the spectrum at 12 kHz (Nfft​=4096) eliminates the upper harmonic air band. However, the fundamental transient energy of hi-hats sits between 5 kHz and 10 kHz, and snare wire crispness peaks between 3 kHz and 7 kHz.

For drum-oriented onset detection and instrument isolation, Nfft​=4096 with 1024 linear bins is the superior representation. The gain in low-frequency separation (11.7 Hz/bin) is essential for separating low-mid percussive elements, whereas the loss of energy above 12 kHz does not materially degrade percussive onset detection, as the dominant transient energy resides well within the 0−12 kHz envelope.

A dual-path architecture is recommended:

  • Path A (Surgical Isolation): Employs the 1024-bin linear Spectrum for user-defined 2D region masks and multi-feature instrument classification.

  • Path B (Broadband Tempo Induction): Aggregates linear bins into a log-spaced or Mel-scaled representation to calculate global onset strength signals across perceptual critical bands.

Role of Time-Domain PCM Data

The PCM port exposes 16384 stereo frames (~341 ms at 48 kHz). While Short-Time Fourier Transforms (STFT) suffer from a windowing latency governed by Nfft​ (85.3 ms for Nfft​=4096), the time-domain waveform contains instantaneous amplitude information.

Time-domain processing cannot replace spectral analysis for instrument isolation because it lacks frequency selectivity. However, a parallel time-domain path operating on the raw PCM buffer provides a zero-latency micro-transient trigger. Computing a short-term root-mean-square (RMS) energy derivative over a 64-sample sliding window (1.33 ms) yields a high-precision temporal mark. When a spectral region detector fires, its timestamp can be sharpened by snapping it to the nearest time-domain energy peak within the current audio frame, reducing temporal jitter from ±5.33 ms (the hop size) down to sub-millisecond precision.

Window and Hop Dynamics

The hop size is fixed by the producer at Mhop​=256 samples (5.33 ms at 48 kHz, or ∼187.5 hops/second). An Nfft​=4096 window corresponds to 85.3 ms of audio data. This temporal overlap means that a sharp percussive transient will spread across approximately 16 consecutive STFT frames in magnitude.

While window length dictates frequency resolution and time-smearing of the magnitude response, the onset timing accuracy is bound by the hop size (5.33 ms). To overcome window-induced time-smearing, onset detection functions must evaluate first-order temporal differences across consecutive hops rather than static frame energy.

In the absence of complex FFT outputs (phase information), onset detection functions must rely entirely on magnitude dynamics across time and frequency. Adaptive Spectral Whitening

The failure of fixed global thresholds and static band splits stems from spectral roll-off: low-frequency energy in audio mixes is orders of magnitude larger than high-frequency energy. A threshold low enough to pick up a hi-hat transient will over-trigger on kick drum rumble4.

Adaptive whitening scales each spectral bin dynamically based on its recent peak history, allowing quiet high-frequency events to compete equally with loud low-frequency events4. The formulation, adapted for real-time execution from Stowell & Plumbley (2007), updates a running peak profile Pn,k​ for frame n and bin k4:

Pn,k​=max(r⋅Pn−1,k​,∣Xn,k​∣)

where r∈(0,1) is a memory decay factor (typically set to r≈0.992, yielding a relaxation half-life of approximately 80 hops or ∼420 ms)6. The whitened spectrum Yn,k​ is calculated as:

Yn,k​=max(Pn,k​,ϵ)∣Xn,k​∣​

where ϵ=10−4 prevents division by zero in silent bands6.

Adaptive whitening compresses the dynamic range per bin to [0.0,1.0]. It eliminates the need for manual per-lane threshold floors, prevents bass dominance, and normalizes the onset strength signal across tracks recorded at different master loudness levels4.

SuperFlux Mechanics

Standard spectral flux computes frame-to-frame differences per bin. When an instrument exhibits vibrato or pitch bends, spectral energy shifts across neighboring bins, generating false positive flux spikes3. The SuperFlux algorithm proposed by Böck et al. solves this by tracking local spectral trajectories using a maximum filter over neighboring bins in the previous frame1:

Dn,k​=max(0,Yn,k​−m∈[−M,M]max​Yn−1,k+m​)

where M is the filter radius (typically M=2 bins)3. If energy at bin k simply drifted from bin k−1 due to vibrato, maxm​Yn−1,k+m​ accounts for it, resulting in Dn,k​≈0. If a genuine percussive attack occurs, new energy floods all bins simultaneously, producing a strong positive differential across Dn,k​3.

Peak-Picking and Latency Mechanics

Peak-picking converts a continuous 1D Onset Detection Function O[n] into binary trigger events. Simple fixed thresholding fails due to local dynamic shifts. The optimal causal peak-picking strategy uses a moving median combined with a local mean threshold3:

δ[n]=Median(O[n−K…n])+λ⋅Mean(O[n−K…n])+α

An onset event is registered at frame n if and only if:

  1. O[n]>δ[n] (Exceeds dynamic adaptive threshold)

  2. O[n]≥O[n−1] and O[n]≥O[n+1] (Local maximum condition)

  3. n−nlast​>τrefractory​ (Refractory lockout period)

To evaluate local maximum condition O[n]≥O[n+1], a causal lookahead of exactly 1 hop (5.33 ms) is introduced. This single-hop delay is imperceptible for live visual performance (well below the 16.6 ms frame budget of a 60 Hz display) but eliminates over 80% of double-trigger artifacts3. Refractory periods τrefractory​ are maintained independently per instrument lane (e.g., 120 ms for kick, 80 ms for snare, 40 ms for hi-hats).

Region-Based Instrument Isolation Architecture

2D Spectrogram Region Masking

A core defect of conventional band-splitting is treating spectral selection as contiguous 1D frequency slices. In complex audio mixes, percussive instruments are rarely confined to a single band, nor do they occupy the entirety of a contiguous frequency range.

The visual configuration interface allows users to define arbitrary 2D Regions of Interest (ROIs) directly on the scrolling spectrogram. Regions isolate specific spectral phenomena: hi-hats in high-frequency broad bands, snare wire snap in mid-high bands, snare body in tonal mid-frequency peaks, and kick drum energy in low-frequency blocks. A region Ri​ is defined by a 2D bounding structure over frequency bins k∈[kmin​,kmax​] and a weighted spectral kernel Wi​(k):

Oi​[n]=k=kmin​∑kmax​​Wi​(k)⋅Dn,k​

To isolate complex instruments, regions support four profile shapes:

  1. Rectangular Mask: Standard uniform weighting Wi​(k)=1.0.

  2. Gaussian Kernel Mask: Centered at fundamental frequency k0​ with bandwidth σ: Wi​(k)=exp(−2σ2(k−k0​)2​).

  3. Comb Harmonic Mask: Series of sparse Gaussian peaks at integer multiples m⋅k0​ for isolating tonal percussion (toms, cowbells, woodblocks).

  4. Freeform Multi-Band Mask: Bitmask buffer enabling disjoint frequency selection (e.g., selecting 150−250 Hz AND 3−5 kHz simultaneously for snare body + snare snap).

Beyond Band-Limiting: Harmonic-Percussive Source Separation

When a snare drum and a bass guitar overlap in the 150−250 Hz region, band-limiting alone fails because the sustained bass note keeps the regional flux high. Harmonic-Percussive Source Separation (HPSS) isolates transient percussive events from sustained harmonic sounds by exploiting their orthogonal spatial orientation on a spectrogram: harmonic elements form horizontal lines across time, while percussive attacks form vertical lines across frequency.

In a real-time HLSL compute shader operating over a 64-hop rolling buffer, HPSS is implemented via 2D median filtering8:

  1. Harmonic Spectrum (Hn,k​): Apply a 1D median filter along the time axis (e.g., median over 9 consecutive hops at bin k).

  2. Percussive Spectrum (Pn,k​): Apply a 1D median filter along the frequency axis (e.g., median over 17 consecutive bins at frame n).

  3. Mask Generation: Compute a soft percussive mask MP​(n,k)=Pn,k2​+Hn,k2​+ϵPn,k2​​.

Multiplying the whitened differential spectrum Dn,k​ by MP​(n,k) strips away sustained bass guitar notes and vocal pads, leaving only percussive transients within the selected region.

Multi-Feature Classifier for Overlapping Instruments

To distinguish a snare drum hit from a simultaneously occurring kick drum or synth pluck within an overlapping frequency region, the detector extracts a 4D feature vector Fi​[n] per region at each hop:

  1. Log Energy Differential (F1​): Magnitude of the whitened flux within the region.

  2. Spectral Centroid (F2​): Center of mass of regional energy:

    F2​=∑k​Yn,k​∑k​k⋅Yn,k​​

  3. Spectral Flatness / Wiener Entropy (F3​): Ratio of geometric mean to arithmetic mean, measuring noise-like (snare wires) versus tonal (kick drum) character:

    F3​=K1​∑Yn,k​exp(K1​∑log(Yn,k​+ϵ))​

  4. Temporal Decay Rate (F4​): Ratio of regional energy at frame n+2 to frame n, measuring transient sharpness.

A snare drum exhibits high spectral flatness (F3​→1.0), a high spectral centroid (F2​), and rapid decay (F4​). A kick drum exhibits low spectral flatness (F3​→0.0) and a low centroid (F2​). Evaluating a weighted dot product Wsnare​⋅Fi​[n]>θ discriminates snare attacks even when kick and snare land on the exact same frame.

Lateral Competitive Inhibition Network

When 8 to 16 region detectors run concurrently, a loud transient (such as a full-kit crash/snare impact) can cause energy to spill across adjacent frequency regions, causing false positive triggers across multiple lanes simultaneously.

To eliminate cross-lane contamination, an Inhibitory Cross-Talk Network is applied prior to peak-picking. Given N active regions with raw onset strengths O1​,O2​,…,ON​, an updated strength O^i​ is computed via lateral inhibition:

O^i​=max​0,Oi​−j=i∑​βj,i​⋅Oj​​

where βj,i​∈[0,1] represents the overlap matrix coefficient between region j and region i. If a primary kick region (j=1) fires with massive energy, it suppresses secondary leakage in the adjacent low-mid snare body region (i=2), ensuring that only the dominant, intended instrument fires its output trigger.

The Comb Filter Matrix (CFM), popularized by Scheirer and refined by Adam Stark (BTrack)9, is selected as the optimal GPU architecture. Unlike autocorrelation—which measures simple lag similarity and suffers during syncopated rhythms—a bank of comb filter resonators actively reinforces periodic pulse trains while attenuating non-periodic off-beat events9.

Octave and Metrical Disambiguation

The dominant failure mode in automated tempo tracking is the "octave error"—selecting half-time (e.g., 60 BPM) or double-time (e.g., 240 BPM) instead of the true tempo (120 BPM)11. CFM output exhibits secondary resonance peaks at integer multiples (τ,2τ,3τ) and sub-multiples (2τ​,3τ​) of the true beat period9.

To enforce metrical stability, two corrective layers are applied:

  1. Log-Gaussian Tempo Prior: A perceptual preference curve P(bpm) peaked at 120 BPM (representing the human motor-preference cadence for rhythmic tapping) weights the raw comb filter energy C(τ)12:

    P(τ)=exp(−2σtempo2​(log2​(bpm(τ))−log2​(120))2​)

    where σtempo​=0.8 octaves12.

  2. Harmonic Peak Suppression Matrix: For each candidate lag τ, its resonant energy C(τ) is adjusted by evaluating its harmonic sub-lags12:

    C^(τ)=C(τ)−γ(C(2τ​)+C(2τ))

    This suppresses double-time and half-time false resonances, isolating the primary tempo node.

Dynamic Tracking, Tempo Shifts, and Confidence Collapse

Live DJ sets feature pitch-bending, gradual tempo ramps, and sudden breakdown sections. The tempo estimation engine tracks these dynamics through two metrics:

  1. Agility Parameter (αcomb​): The feedback parameter in the recursive comb update equation governs memory retention9:

    yt​(τ)=αcomb​⋅yt−τ​(τ)+(1−αcomb​)⋅O[t]

    Setting αcomb​=0.88 provides rapid tracking of live pitch bends (±5 BPM over 2 seconds)9.

  2. Honest Confidence Metric (Qtempo​): Calculated as the Normalized Peak-to-Average Ratio (NPAR) of the comb filter output vector C^:

    Qtempo​=StdDev(C^)maxτ​C^(τ)−Mean(C^)​

    During quiet breakdowns, ambient noise, or non-rhythmic passages, Qtempo​ drops toward zero. When Qtempo​<θconf​, the module transitions into a Free-Wheel Hold State, freezing the estimated BPM at its last trusted value rather than decaying into uncalibrated noise or hallucinating incorrect tempos.

Beat Tracking, Phase Alignment, and Predictive Scheduling

Evaluation of Causal Beat Tracking Architectures

Tempo estimation provides the rate (BPM), but driving synchronised visuals requires tracking the precise beat phase ϕ(t)∈[0.0,1.0)—determining exactly when the next beat will occur9. Non-causal beat trackers (such as offline Viterbi dynamic programming) inspect future audio frames to place beats, making them unsuitable for live processing15.

Four causal beat-tracking architectures were evaluated:

  1. Coupled Oscillator / Phase-Locked Loop (PLL): Continuous phase accumulator driven by predicted tempo, phase-corrected by weighted onset triggers14.

  2. Adam Stark Comb Filter Matrix (BTrack): Maintains a 2D matrix X(τ,θ) representing tempo period τ and phase offset θ simultaneously9.

  3. Multi-Agent Tracking (BeatRoot IBT): Tracks multiple competing tempo/phase hypothesis threads12.

  4. Bounded-Horizon Causal Dynamic Programming (Ellis Adaptation): Forward-only Viterbi pass over a sliding 2-second onset buffer12.

The Dual-Loop Beat Phase-Locked Loop (PLL), augmented by continuous phase alignment from the Comb Filter Matrix, provides the optimal combination of minimal execution overhead and robust phase stability.

Comb Filter Matrix Mechanics on GPU

The Comb Filter Matrix X(τ,θ) represents tempo period τ∈[τmin​,τmax​] along the vertical axis and phase offset θ∈[0,τ−1] along the horizontal axis9. At each audio hop t, the matrix state is updated in parallel across a 2D GPU execution grid9:

X(τ,θτ,t​)←αcfm​⋅O[t]+(1−αcfm​)⋅m,nmax​(cτ​(τm​)⋅cθ​(θn​)⋅X(τm​,θn​))

where cτ​ and cθ​ are Gaussian transition weights that allow energy to propagate smoothly across adjacent tempo and phase bins as speed varies9. The location of the absolute maximum in X(τ,θ) explicitly defines both the dominant tempo period τ∗ and the exact current phase alignment θ∗.

Dual-Loop PLL Law and Predictive Visual Scheduling

To drive visual rendering engines, the module must output a continuous 60 Hz phase variable ϕ(t)∈[0.0,1.0) that increments steadily between beats and reaches 1.0 (wrapping to 0.0) exactly on the beat point.

The PLL maintains an internal phase state ϕpll​ and an internal beat period Tpll​ (in seconds). At each cook frame Δt:

ϕpll​←ϕpll​+Tpll​Δt​

When a strong onset trigger arrives from a designated primary rhythm lane (e.g., kick drum or isolated snare) at observed phase ϕobs​, a phase error is calculated:

Δϕ=Wrap[−0.5,0.5]​(ϕobs​−ϕpll​)

The error corrects both the phase state and the underlying period through two feedback loops:

  1. Immediate Phase Correction: ϕpll​←ϕpll​+μphase​⋅Δϕ

  2. Subtle Period Correction: Tpll​←Tpll​+μtempo​⋅Δϕ

Setting μphase​=0.15 and μtempo​=0.02 ensures that single spurious onsets (e.g., syncopated drum fills) cause minimal disruption, while sustained tempo or phase shifts pull the oscillator lock within two bars14.

Inter-Cook State Persistence and Asynchronous Hop Handling

Modules cook at display rate (fcook​≈60 Hz), while audio hops arrive from the engine at fhop​≈187.5 Hz. Consequently, each cook cycle must process a variable number of incoming audio hops—typically ΔNhops​=⌊60187.5​⌋=3 hops, with an occasional 4-hop burst.

To prevent double-counting or losing hops, state management relies on tracking an engine generation counter:

  1. Generation Delta Tracking: Uniform inputs provide _DataN_Generation. The shader maintains LastProcessedGeneration in a persistent structured buffer.

  2. Loop Iteration: Compute Pass 1 through Pass 3 execute an inner loop over h=0…(CurrentGen−LastProcessedGen−1).

  3. Ring Buffer Pointer Management: The onset history ring buffer uses atomic modulo math:

    WriteIndex=(HistoryHead+h)(mod800)

    No memory copying of historical data is ever performed; updates write directly to the circular write head.

During render hitches or frame drops (e.g., cook rate dropping to 20 Hz), ΔNhops​ may increase to 10–12 hops. The inner loop processes all backlogged hops sequentially in a single cook, maintaining complete phase continuity without temporal drift.

Precision and Numerical Stability

Long-running compute passes operating over days of continuous live performance encounter float32 precision breakdown if counters or phase accumulators grow unbounded.

  1. Phase Wrapping: Beat phase variables are strictly wrapped using fractional bounds: ϕ←frac(ϕ).

  2. Running Peak Relaxation: Bin peaks Pn,k​ are clamped to a minimum lower floor ϵfloor​=10−4 and an absolute upper ceiling 104.

  3. Logarithmic Stabilization: All dynamic range log transforms utilize log(1.0+γ⋅X) to prevent negative infinity evaluations near zero magnitude.

Spectrogram User Interface and Interactive Configuration

Visual Display Transform and Dynamic Range Mapping

The spectrogram interface serves as the visual control deck for configuring region masks. Rendering raw magnitude STFT data directly results in an unusable interface: low-frequency rumble blooms into a solid block, while high-frequency hi-hats remain invisible.

To make all instruments simultaneously visible and clickable, the display pipeline transforms raw magnitude X(n,k) through three successive stages:

  1. Logarithmic Frequency Rebinning: The vertical display axis maps linearly to perceptual pitch:

    ypixel​(k)=log2​(kmax​)−log2​(kmin​)log2​(k)−log2​(kmin​)​

  2. Per-Bin Dynamic Peak Equalization: Bins are normalized by their running peak Pn,k​, elevating hi-hats and cymbal snap to equal visual prominence with the bass drum.

  3. Power-Law Dynamic Compression: Magnitude values are mapped to visual brightness via Sdisp​=(Yn,k​)γ, where γ=0.45 expands quiet transient details above the background noise floor.

Color mapping utilizes high-perceptual-contrast palettes (such as Magma or Inferno), where black represents silence, deep red represents sustained harmonic background, bright yellow represents percussive transients, and pure white marks active onset detection triggers.

Interactive Region Selection and Real-Time Feedback Overlay

Users configure instrument isolation by interacting directly with the spectrogram canvas:

  1. 2D Region Selection: Click-and-drag interaction creates rectangular or elliptical 2D bounds over the 64-hop log-frequency display. A context menu allows assigning selected regions to specific control output channels (e.g., Kick_Trigger, Snare_Trigger, Hat_Trigger).

  2. Live Waveform & Threshold Overlay: Suspended inside or adjacent to each region box, a mini-trace renders the real-time regional flux signal Oi​[n] alongside the dynamic threshold line δi​[n].

  3. Trigger Flash Feedback: When a region fires a valid onset, its border flashes white, and an animated trigger pulse fires across the output bus, providing immediate visual verification without requiring audio monitoring.

  4. Interactive "Learn Region" Affordance: The user clicks a "Learn Instrument" button and taps an instrument hit on the live input. The system captures a 3-frame STFT snapshot around the hit, isolates bins whose flux spikes >3σ above the mean, and automatically generates a fitted 2D multi-band region mask around the target instrument.

Evaluation, Ground Truth, and Automated Scoring

Quantitative Evaluation Metrics

To eliminate subjective manual tuning, algorithm changes must be evaluated against standardized mathematical criteria1.

1. Onset Detection Metrics

Given ground-truth onset timestamps Tgt​ and detected onset timestamps Tdet​, an onset is classified as a True Positive (TP) if ∣tdet​−tgt​∣≤Δw, where Δw=±25 ms is the standard evaluation window1. Unmatched detections are False Positives (FP), and missed ground-truth hits are False Negatives (FN)1.

Precision (P)=TP+FPTP​,Recall (R)=TP+FNTP​
F1​-Measure=2⋅P+RP⋅R​

2. Beat Tracking Metrics

Beat accuracy is evaluated using continuity metrics:

  • CMLc (Correct Metrical Level continuous): Percentage of beats tracked continuously within a ±17.5% phase window at the correct tempo.

  • AMLc (Allowed Metrical Level continuous): Percentage of beats tracked continuously allowing for half/double-time metrical variations.

Synthesized Conclusions

The architecture specified in this report transforms audio reactivity in Sentinel from a heuristic, hand-tuned visualization tool into an accurate, mathematically deterministic audio analysis engine.

By replacing fixed Mel-band splits with Adaptive Spectral Whitening and SuperFlux differentials, per-bin dynamic range issues are eliminated, enabling quiet hi-hats and loud kick drums to be tracked simultaneously without threshold starvation3.

By advancing from 1D frequency splitting to 2D Multi-Feature Region Isolation bolstered by Harmonic-Percussive Source Separation, broadband instruments like snares can be isolated cleanly from dense commercial mixes.

Finally, by mapping a Comb Filter Matrix and a dual-loop Phase-Locked Loop directly onto parallel HLSL compute passes, tempo tracking achieves octave stability and sub-frame phase locking while executing well within a single millisecond of GPU time9.

This design meets all real-time visual constraints, operates deterministically without neural network latency, and establishes a robust framework for every future audio-reactive project built on Sentinel.
